Vegetarian diet that fits a student's time and budget.

THE PROBLEM

Vegetarian protein advice online splits into two useless extremes: meal plans that assume unlimited time and a stocked pantry, or generic "eat more lentils" tips with no plan attached. As an incoming MBA student about to be short on both time and money, I wanted something that told me what to cook this week with what I could afford — without assuming my only cuisine was the one tied to my name.

WHO IT'S FOR

Time-poor, budget-conscious vegetarians — students especially — who know roughly what "more protein" means but not how to turn that into a grocery list and a Tuesday-night dinner.

KEY DECISIONS

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

Today — progress against targets, and what to eat next

Log meals — built-in meals or anything you ate yourself

Groceries — budget-aware plan, built from what you already own

Swaps — easy fixes for carb-heavy meals

01

Optimize the grocery list, not the recipe

The real cost lever isn't any single recipe's price — it's ingredient overlap. Once you've bought a bag of lentils, every extra serving from it is nearly free. So the planner doesn't rank meals by individual cost; it greedily builds a basket that maximizes meals-per-dollar across shared ingredients, inside a weekly budget.

02

Personalization that resists stereotyping

Early versions let someone pick favorite cuisines and just boosted those — which meant picking "Indian" got you only Indian food, flattening a preference into an assumption. I added a decay instead: each repeat of a cuisine within the week lowers its score for the next pick, so favorites are weighted up but variety is structural, not optional.

03

Weekly shortfall steers daily picks

Nutrient targets that reset every day make a bad Tuesday invisible by Wednesday. Pulse instead tracks the week-to-date shortfall and lets it bias same-day suggestions — behind on fiber this week, and today's top pick leans fiber-heavy, not just protein-heavy.

WHAT I'D DO WITH MORE TIME

Real nutrition data (USDA FoodData Central) and local store prices instead of hand-estimated values; a larger recipe library across more cuisines; accounts and sync, which need a hosted database; and talking to five or ten classmates to pressure-test whether the grocery optimizer actually changes what they buy, not just what they say they'd buy.
