Pulse: hitting protein goals as a broke, busy, vegetarian student

An AI-native build, start to live product, in a single week of evenings.

$0hosting cost

The problem

Vegetarian protein advice online defaults to one of two things: meal plans that assume unlimited time and a stocked pantry, or generic "eat more lentils" tips with no plan attached. As an incoming MBA student about to be short on both time and money, I wanted something that told me, specifically, what to cook this week with what I could actually afford — and that didn't assume my only cuisine was the one tied to my name.

Who it's for

Time-poor, budget-conscious vegetarians — students especially — who know roughly what "more protein" means but not how to turn that into a grocery list and a Tuesday-night dinner.

Key decisions

Optimize the grocery list, not just the meal

The real cost lever isn't a single recipe's price — it's ingredient overlap. Once you've bought a bag of lentils, every extra serving from it is nearly free. So the planner doesn't rank meals by individual cost; it greedily builds a basket that maximizes meals-per-dollar across shared ingredients, respecting a weekly budget.

Cuisine personalization that resists stereotyping

Early versions let a user pick favorite cuisines and just boosted those. The problem: someone who picks "Indian" would get only Indian food — flattening them to one assumption. I added a decay function instead: each repeat of a cuisine within the week lowers its score for the next suggestion, so favorites are weighted up but variety is structurally preserved rather than left to chance.

Let weekly gaps steer daily suggestions

Nutrient targets reset daily in most trackers, which means a bad Tuesday is invisible by Wednesday. Pulse instead looks at the week-to-date shortfall and lets it bias same-day suggestions — if you're behind on fiber for the week, today's top pick leans fiber-heavy, not just protein-heavy.

What I'd do with more time

Real nutrition data (USDA FoodData Central) and local store prices instead of hand-estimated values; a larger recipe library across more cuisines; accounts and sync, which need a hosted database; and talking to 5–10 classmates to pressure-test whether the grocery optimizer actually changes what they buy, not just what they say they'd buy.
